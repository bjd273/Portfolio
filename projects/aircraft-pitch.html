Chasing a response shape, not just a number

Left alone, this airplane's short-period pitch mode is lightly damped — the real damping ratio, from the actual model, comes out to about 0.42. That's the kind of mode a pilot feels as a wallow: it gets where it's going, but not cleanly. The point of a pitch-rate controller isn't just to reach a commanded rate; it's to get there with a damping ratio a pilot would actually want, without ever demanding more elevator deflection than the airplane has.

The plant here is the genuine article — a linearized short-period model, two states (angle of attack and pitch rate), driven through a real first-order actuator lag at 31.4 rad/s, with elevator travel capped at the airplane's real ±20° limit. The design target isn't an arbitrary shape either: it's the same plant, re-poled to a damping ratio of 0.8 at its own natural frequency — "the airplane, but better damped" — which is exactly the reference this page's controller is judged against.

plant: ẋ = Ax + Bδe, x = [α, q], δ̇e = −31.4(δe − u) natural frequency: ωn ≈ 7.10 rad/s open-loop damping: ζ ≈ 0.42 ideal model: same plant, re-poled to ζ = 0.8 at the same ωn

The controller tracks the commanded rate almost exactly — by design, the integral state in its own equations guarantees zero steady-state error to a constant command. What it can't promise for free is elevator authority: push for a faster response and the elevator works harder to get there, right up against that ±20° ceiling.

One honest substitution: the original design is an H∞ mixed-sensitivity synthesis — four weighting filters (tracking error, control effort, sensor noise, elevator disturbance) shaping the loop directly in the frequency domain, solved by hinfsyn. That's a real optimization problem that needs its own specialized machinery, not something to approximate from scratch. This page uses full-state LQR with integral action instead — a different, exact optimal-control method, the same one already verified on the active-suspension page — tuned by a single control-effort penalty rather than four frequency-shaped weights. The qualitative story the original design explores — tighter tracking costs more high-frequency control effort — comes through the same way.
