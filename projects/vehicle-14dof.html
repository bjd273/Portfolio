What fourteen degrees of freedom actually buys you

A full vehicle model — the kind used to validate a real suspension or a stability-control system before it ever touches a test track — doesn't stop at "the car turns when you steer it." The reference model this page is built from carries fourteen degrees of freedom: six for the body (moving and rotating in three dimensions), one heave state for each of the four wheels, and one spin state for each wheel again. Underneath all fourteen, the part that actually decides how the car behaves is the same four contact patches doing the same nonlinear thing tires always do.

This page doesn't run all fourteen. What it runs is real: the vehicle's actual mass (1,181 kg), yaw inertia (2,066 kg·m²), and the exact distances from its center of gravity to each axle (1.515 m front, 1.504 m rear) — extracted directly from the reference model's own data, not textbook stand-ins. Left out, and said plainly: the suspension's heave and roll, and each wheel's own spin dynamics. What's left is the classic three-state "bicycle model" — lateral velocity, yaw rate, and the tire physics in between:

m(v̇y + vxr) = Fyfcosδ + Fyr Izṙ = a·Fyfcosδ − b·Fyr αf = δ − atan((vy+ar)/vx) αr = −atan((vy−br)/vx)

The part that makes this worth building at all is Fy(α) — the tire's lateral force as a function of slip angle. The easy version of this model uses a straight line: force proportional to slip angle, forever. Real tires don't. They build force quickly at first, peak somewhere around 6–10° of slip, and then give some back as the rubber starts to slide instead of grip. The curve on this page is the vehicle's own Magic Formula (Pacejka) tire data, pulled from the same reference model — the same one used for both axles, since this vehicle's own configuration doesn't specify a different front and rear tire.

Turn the wheel further, and the yaw-rate chart tells the real story: it climbs, peaks within the first ten degrees or so of steering angle, and then starts falling even as you keep adding lock. That's the tire curve talking. Past its grip peak, more steering angle just adds slip angle — heat, not turning.

One piece of the tire model is a disclosed simplification rather than the full reference formula: the curve's steepness near zero slip (B, in Magic-Formula terms) is derived here from the tire's real cornering-stiffness coefficients (PKY1, PKY2) at each axle's actual static load, rather than the complete load- and camber-dependent formula the full reference tire model uses. The curve's shape and peak — where the interesting behavior lives — are the vehicle's own numbers.
