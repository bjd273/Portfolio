Response to the same bump

An actuator that knows exactly how hard to push

A passive suspension is a compromise struck once, in the spring rate and the damper, and it can't change its mind mid-bump. An active suspension adds one more input — a force actuator between the body and the wheel — and a controller that decides, at every instant, how hard to push. The question this page answers isn't whether that helps. It's how you decide, mathematically, how hard is too hard.

The quarter-car model itself is the exact one from the original design: a 350 kg body mass on a spring (16,000 N/m) and damper (1,000 N·s/m) above a 30 kg wheel mass, which meets the road through the tire's own stiffness (190,000 N/m). Four states — body position and velocity, wheel position and velocity — evolve under one exogenous input (the road) and one controlled input (the actuator force):

That's a linear–quadratic regulator: penalize the states you care about (here, body acceleration and suspension deflection, folded into Q) and the control effort you're willing to spend (R), and solve for the state-feedback gain that minimizes the total cost forever. The solution comes from the algebraic Riccati equation — the same equation, worked by hand for a 4×4 system, whose numerical solve is described below.

Every gain this page computes was checked, numerically, before it ever reached the page: the closed-loop system is confirmed stable at every setting, and the Riccati residual is driven below 10⁻ⁱ before the gain is trusted. An optimal controller you haven't verified is just a controller you're hoping is optimal.

The slider doesn't choose between two fixed personalities — it continuously re-solves the Riccati equation for a different control-effort penalty R, live, every time you move it. Toward Comfort, R is large: the actuator is expensive to use, so the optimal law barely touches it, and the ride stays close to passive. Toward Sport, R is small: force is cheap, so the controller leans on the actuator hard, flattening suspension travel — measurably, watch the chart — at the cost of sharper, less comfortable body accelerations. There's no setting that wins on both; that trade is the entire content of the R parameter.

One honest substitution: the original design compares H∞ and H2 mixed-sensitivity synthesis, which shape the response across frequency bands directly. Those solvers need bilinear matrix optimization that isn't something to approximate from scratch. This page uses full-state LQR instead — a different, exact optimal-control method, not a stand-in for the same one — solved here by integrating the Riccati differential equation forward via 4th-order Runge–Kutta until it reaches its steady, algebraic solution.
