Why the best move depends on your last one

Point-and-click path planning assumes the robot goes where it's told. Here it doesn't — every step has a real chance of sliding into the other direction instead, and that chance depends on whether the robot is continuing straight or just changed its mind. Planning around that isn't optional; it's the entire problem.

The grid is 41×41. The robot can only move right or up. At every cell, the question is: given where I am, and given what I just did, which action has the higher expected value from here to the goal? That's a backward-induction problem — start at the goal, work backward one time step at a time, and let each step's answer lean on the next step's already-solved answer:

V_t(s) = max[a ∈ {→, ↑}] p(a) · [r(s_a) + V_t+1(s_a)] + (1−p(a)) · [r(s_ā) + V_t+1(s_ā)] p(a) = 0.80 if a matches the previous move (momentum) p(a) = 0.60 if a is a direction change (the slip) s_a = the cell reached if the intended action succeeds s_ā = the cell reached if it slips into the other action instead

This is the twist that makes it interesting: the transition probabilities aren't fixed, they depend on momentum. The recursion above has to loop over three possible "what did I just do" states for every cell before it can even ask which action is best — the policy at 80%/60% looks nothing like the policy you'd get by ignoring momentum entirely.

The live counters above aren't decoration — they're the same Monte Carlo verification from the original coursework, just running continuously instead of stopping at 1,000 trials, tallying every attempt in the background: obstacles hit, boundaries run off, goals reached. The route drawn on screen is always one of the successful ones — a real sampled run, just filtered so what you're watching never wanders off and dead-ends. The success-rate counter is where the failures still show up, honestly.

It's a different flavor of the same instinct running through the rest of this collection: don't just derive an answer — watch it survive contact with the randomness it promised to handle. Here that's a Bellman recursion instead of a μ-analysis, but the discipline is identical: solve exactly, then stress-test relentlessly.
