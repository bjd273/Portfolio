What actually limits acceleration

Two limits, and a crossover between them

A motor running at constant power delivers torque inversely proportional to speed — twice the RPM, half the torque, same power. Taken literally, that means infinite torque at zero speed. No real drivetrain gets that, and the reason isn't the motor. It's the tire.

At any given acceleration, some of the car's weight shifts rearward — the classic weight-transfer effect, driven by the car's actual center-of-gravity height (0.459 m) and wheelbase (1.918 m). Whatever normal load is left on the driven wheels, multiplied by the tire-road friction coefficient, is the hardest the tire can push before it starts to slip. The real, usable drive force at any speed is the smaller of two numbers — what the motor's power curve can deliver, and what the tire can put down:

Fdrive(v) = min( P/v , μ·Rf(a) ) Rf(a) = (W/L)·(b − a·h/g) — front normal load under acceleration a Fresist(v) = μrr·mg + ½·Cd·ρ·A·v² — rolling + aerodynamic drag

Watch the force chart: at low speed the car rides the traction-limit line, not the power curve — all the motor's torque in the world wouldn't help, the tire would just spin. Only once speed climbs enough for the power-limited curve to drop below the traction ceiling does the car actually start using all its horsepower. Every real launch works this way, electric or not.

Two things are corrected here relative to the original calculation, both worth stating plainly. First, the original computed time-to-speed as speed ÷ acceleration-at-that-speed — treating the final acceleration as if it applied for the whole run, rather than integrating the actual, changing acceleration from a stop. This page instead solves the real equation of motion, m·dv/dt = Fdrive(v) − Fresist(v), forward in time. Second, the original's constant-power torque formula was evaluated starting from 0 RPM, where it's mathematically infinite; the traction limit above is what actually caps it, the way it does on a real car.

The car's real top speed — where drive force and resistance finally balance and acceleration hits zero — comes out well past highway speed for these numbers, a direct consequence of how little aerodynamic drag area (0.163 m²) buys a vehicle shaped like this one.
